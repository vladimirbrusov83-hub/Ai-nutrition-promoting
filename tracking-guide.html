Tracking Guide

How to Track Food
Accurately

The questions every client asks in week one. Answered once, clearly, so you can track with confidence.

1
The One Rule That Fixes Everything

Most tracking confusion comes from one question: which state do I weigh this in? Raw or cooked? Before or after boiling? Whole or prepped?

The answer is almost always the same: the nutrition label describes the food in its dry, uncooked, unprocessed state. That's the weight the database was built from. That's what you track.

When you boil pasta, it absorbs water. That water has no calories. If you weigh cooked pasta and log it as "pasta," you're dramatically undercounting — because 230g of cooked pasta is only about 100g of dry pasta, but the label says 100g = 350 kcal. You'd be logging 350 kcal when you actually ate 350 kcal worth. Sounds right — but only if you weighed it dry. Weigh it cooked and enter the same 350 kcal per 100g, and your number is completely wrong.

The rule: Track food in the state the label describes. For pasta, rice, oats, and most grains — that's always dry. For fresh meat — that's always raw.
2
Raw vs Cooked: The Big Four

Here's exactly what happens to the most commonly tracked foods during cooking — and what that means for your log.

Food Before cooking After cooking Track as
Pasta (dry) 100g ~230g cooked Dry
White rice 100g ~290g cooked Dry
Oats (rolled or steel-cut) 100g ~240g cooked Dry
Chicken breast 165g raw ~120g cooked Raw
Ground beef (80/20) 113g raw ~85g cooked Raw
Eggs — Negligible difference Either

Chicken loses 25–30% of its weight when cooked — that's water, not calories. The calories stay. So 165g of raw chicken breast ≈ 120g cooked, but the protein and calories are the same either way. Just pick one method and stick to it.

Ground beef loses both water and fat when cooked, which means cooked ground beef has a different macro ratio than raw. Always track raw, before cooking.

Eggs are the one exception where it genuinely doesn't matter — the water loss is minimal enough to ignore. Log a whole large egg as ~50g regardless of how it's cooked.

Already cooked and forgot to weigh it raw? Search your tracker for "chicken breast cooked" or "rice cooked" — most apps have a separate entry for the cooked state. Use that, don't guess.
3
Reading a Nutrition Label
Start here every time: Check the serving size before anything else. Every number on the label is relative to that amount — not the whole package.

Per serving vs per 100g. "Per 100g" lets you compare two products fairly. "Per serving" tells you what you actually eat — but serving sizes are often unrealistically small. Peanut butter lists a serving as 2 tablespoons (32g). Most people eat 50–70g. That difference is 110–165 extra calories per sitting.

Total carbs vs net carbs. Fiber is listed under total carbohydrates but your body doesn't digest it — it passes through. Net carbs = total carbs minus fiber. This only matters practically if you're tracking for ketogenic or very low-carb goals. For most people, just use total carbs.

The "0g trans fat" trap. Labels are legally allowed to round down to zero if a serving contains less than 0.5g of trans fat. That means a product with 0.4g per serving shows "0g." Scan the ingredients list for partially hydrogenated oil — that's trans fat, regardless of what the label says.

Sugar is not separate from carbs. Sugar is already counted inside total carbohydrates. Don't add them together — you'd be double-counting.

Ingredients list = weight order. Ingredients are listed from most to least by weight. If sugar, corn syrup, or a variation of sugar is in the first two or three ingredients, that product is primarily sugar. This is useful when comparing products that look similar on the macro panel.

4
When to Weigh, When to Measure

Not everything needs a gram scale. But some foods are so calorie-dense and so easy to misjudge that you absolutely have to weigh them. Here's the breakdown.

Always weigh (gram scale)
  • Cooking oils and butter
  • Nut butters
  • Cheese
  • Raw meat and fish
  • Oats, rice, pasta (dry)
  • Protein powder
  • Nuts and seeds
Cups / spoons are fine
  • Spices and herbs
  • Leafy greens
  • Whole fruits (log the item)
  • Canned beans (drained)
  • Black coffee
  • Low-calorie condiments

Protein powder is on the weigh list for a specific reason: scoop sizes vary 5–10g between brands and even between batches. A heaping scoop vs a level scoop is a 30–40 kcal swing per serving, repeated twice a day. Weigh it once to calibrate, then you can trust your scoop.

The most common tracking error: eyeballing cooking oil. A "light drizzle" is often 2–3 tablespoons. That's 240–360 hidden calories per meal — enough to completely erase a calorie deficit. Measure it every time.
5
Eating Out

Eating out doesn't have to break your tracking. The approach depends on what kind of restaurant you're at.

Chain restaurants — use their data. McDonald's, Chipotle, Subway, Panera, and most major chains are legally required to publish accurate nutrition information. Your tracking app already has it. Use the official entry, not a generic one.

Chipotle specifically has one of the best nutrition tools in the industry — build your exact bowl in their app before ordering and get a precise macro breakdown. This works because their ingredients are weighed by portion size consistently.

Sit-down restaurants — lead with protein. You can't know exactly what went into a restaurant dish, but you can estimate confidently. Start with the protein source: a grilled chicken breast is usually 150–200g, a salmon fillet 170–200g, a beef medallion 120–150g. Log that weight, then estimate the carb and fat sides. A reasonable estimate beats logging nothing.

Sauces and dressings. Assume 2–3x more than the menu implies. Restaurant portions of sauce, dressing, and oil are consistently larger than people expect. Ask for it on the side — you'll use less, and you can see what you're getting.

The ±100 kcal rule: If you eat out occasionally and land within 100 kcal of your target, that's excellent tracking. Obsessing over precise restaurant macros creates more psychological stress than the inaccuracy costs in progress. Estimate, log, move on.
6
The Silent Calorie Killers

These are the foods that consistently explain the gap between "I tracked everything" and "my results don't match my numbers." They're not exotic — they're the ordinary things almost everyone forgets.

Cooking oil
The most undertracked food in any diet. Spray, drizzle, or pour — it adds up instantly. One tablespoon of olive oil is 120 kcal. Measure it every time with no exceptions.
Coffee additions
Black coffee is zero calories. A latte with oat milk and a pump of syrup is 150–250 kcal before you've eaten anything. Log every milk, cream, and syrup addition.
Bites while cooking
A few tastes, a handful of nuts while plating, finishing what the kids didn't eat — 100–200 invisible calories per day is normal for someone who doesn't log these. Log it or stop doing it.
Alcohol
1g of alcohol contains 7 kcal — more than carbs or protein, less than fat. A pint of beer is 180–250 kcal. A glass of wine is 120–160 kcal. Mixers often double it. Alcohol also temporarily suppresses fat oxidation — it's not just the calories.
Condiments
Ketchup, BBQ sauce, mayo, tahini, ranch, sriracha mayo — most people use 2–4x the serving size on the label. Weigh or measure condiments the same way you would nut butter.
Fruit juice
One 240ml glass of orange juice has the same sugar content as a can of Coke — with almost no fiber to slow absorption. Whole fruit is almost always the better choice: more fiber, more volume, slower digestion.
7
How Accurate Do You Need to Be?
The real standard

±10–15% daily is normal and acceptable. Even food lab analysis of identical meals varies 10–20% between samples. Perfect tracking is not achievable — and it's not the goal.

Consistent beats precise

Tracking the same foods the same way every day compounds over time. A systematic 10% undercount is fine — your body adapts to your actual intake, not your theoretical intake. Inconsistency is what breaks progress, not a small constant error.

When precision matters more

Tighten up when you're in a hard cut (below ~10% body fat for men, ~18% for women), or when progress has genuinely stalled for 3+ weeks with no other explanation. Outside of those situations, relax.

The 80/20 rule for tracking

Get the big items right — protein sources, grains, oils, calorie-dense foods. Don't stress the small ones: a splash of hot sauce, a mint, half a cup of black coffee. Track the things that move the needle. Ignore the things that don't.

"Track to learn, not to be perfect. After a few weeks, you'll know your foods well enough that the scale becomes a check, not a requirement."
